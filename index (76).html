Research Gaps in Skin Tone Diversity for AI-Based Dermatological Diagnosis

Professor, Computer Science and Engineering

Abstract

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has shown considerable promise in dermatology by assisting clinicians in disease diagnosis, lesion classification, and decision support systems. Deep learning techniques have demonstrated performance comparable to dermatologists in several benchmark datasets. However, significant concerns remain regarding the fairness and generalizability of these systems across diverse populations. Existing dermatology datasets frequently contain an overrepresentation of lighter skin tones, resulting in reduced diagnostic accuracy for individuals with darker skin types. Such imbalances may introduce algorithmic bias and increase healthcare disparities. This paper highlights current research gaps associated with skin tone diversity in AI-based dermatological diagnosis and discusses potential future research directions for developing equitable and inclusive AI systems.

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence techniques, particularly deep learning and computer vision methods, have become increasingly important in dermatological diagnosis. Several studies have demonstrated strong performance for skin lesion classification and melanoma detection. However, the reliability of these systems largely depends upon the quality and diversity of training datasets. Most publicly available dermatological image datasets have been collected predominantly from lighter-skinned populations. Consequently, AI systems trained using such datasets may learn patterns specific to limited demographic groups while performing poorly on underrepresented populations.

The underrepresentation of darker skin tones creates significant challenges because skin diseases often present differently across various skin types. Variations in pigmentation can alter visual manifestations, making diagnosis more difficult when training datasets lack sufficient diversity. 3. Research Gaps and Impact of Skin Tone Bias in AI-Based Dermatology

Skin tone bias represents one of the most critical challenges in AI-based dermatological diagnosis. Current evidence indicates that artificial intelligence systems often demonstrate reduced diagnostic accuracy when analyzing darker skin tones, resulting in increased false-negative rates and potentially severe clinical consequences. Since many dermatological AI systems are developed using datasets that disproportionately represent lighter-skinned individuals, these systems frequently fail to recognize essential disease patterns among underrepresented populations.

3.1 Elevated False Negatives and Missed Diagnoses

When AI systems trained predominantly on lighter skin are applied to darker skin populations, their performance declines significantly. Studies evaluating AI dermatology applications among Indian patients reported progressive reductions in diagnostic accuracy with increasing skin pigmentation. One system demonstrated a decrease from nearly 60% accuracy in Fitzpatrick skin type IV patients to approximately 40% among type VI individuals. Such reductions increase the possibility of false-negative outcomes, where serious diseases may remain undetected.

Similarly, evaluations conducted on the "Pipsqueak" melanoma dataset, specifically designed to include darker skin images, demonstrated a sensitivity value as low as 0.11. This resulted in substantial misclassification of malignant melanoma as benign lesions. Although melanoma occurs less frequently among darker-skinned populations, studies suggest poorer prognosis and increased mortality because diagnosis often occurs at later stages.

3.2 Failure to Generalize Across Diverse Populations

A significant challenge in AI-based dermatology is the inability of models to generalize across diverse demographic groups. Models trained using homogeneous datasets generally perform effectively only within those specific populations and frequently experience substantial reductions in performance when evaluated externally. Beyond melanoma detection, similar problems have been observed for conditions such as eczema, vitiligo, and inflammatory skin diseases, where pigmentation differences modify visual disease characteristics.

3.3 Hidden Stratification and Misleading Performance Metrics

The observed disparity can be explained by hidden stratification, a phenomenon where high overall performance metrics conceal failures within minority populations. Models may appear to achieve dermatologist-level performance on benchmark datasets while simultaneously exhibiting poor outcomes among underrepresented skin groups. Such hidden failures may create serious risks during real-world deployment.

3.4 Dataset Representation Challenges

The primary cause of diagnostic disparity is insufficient diversity within existing dermatological datasets. Systematic reviews indicate that fewer than 5% of images in major public dermatological datasets represent Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI. Furthermore, several recently approved medical AI systems have undergone validation using patient populations where darker skin tones remain minimally represented.

3.5 Limitations of Existing Skin Tone Classification Methods

Another limitation involves reliance on the Fitzpatrick skin scale. Although commonly used in dermatological studies, the Fitzpatrick scale was originally developed to measure skin sensitivity to sunlight rather than provide comprehensive characterization of skin pigmentation. Researchers have identified limitations in its use for computer vision applications because it frequently correlates more strongly with self-reported race than objective skin measurements.

To address these limitations, researchers have recommended more comprehensive frameworks such as the Monk Skin Tone Scale and the L'Oreal Color Chart Map. These approaches incorporate a wider range of skin shades and undertones and may improve dataset inclusivity and AI fairness.

3.6 Research Gaps Identified

  • Limited representation of darker skin tones in publicly available datasets.
  • Insufficient external validation across diverse ethnic populations.
  • Heavy dependence on Fitzpatrick classification despite known limitations.
  • Lack of fairness-aware performance evaluation metrics.
  • Limited use of explainable AI methods for identifying prediction bias.
  • Absence of large-scale collaborative datasets from multiple geographic regions.
  • Limited investigation of AI reliability in real-world clinical environments.

4. Future Research Directions

  • Development of globally diverse dermatological datasets with balanced skin tone representation.
  • Integration of fairness-aware machine learning algorithms.
  • Implementation of explainable AI techniques to improve transparency.
  • Adoption of modern multidimensional skin tone measurement frameworks.
  • Creation of multi-center collaborative datasets across different populations.
  • Conducting large-scale real-world clinical validation studies.
